A · DECISIONS & OPEN QUESTIONS

Context bar. Tapping “Right now” expands the bar in place (01) over a blurred deck — you never leave the screen, and Done collapses it back to the pill. It opens expanded the first time each session. Collapsed it shows glyphs only; expanded, words + glyphs.

Size switcher. A detached pill floating under the context bar, right-aligned, with its own shadow — it reads as a filter over the deck rather than part of the bar, and it can slide away on scroll.

Add actions. Two separate pills rather than one split slab: a 1:1 plus circle for a single task on the left, Brain dump taking the width on the right with a brain glyph.

Card bottom rail. Requirements live below the hairline on a faintly tinted panel — size + due on the left, context glyphs on the right. The description now fills what used to be dead space.

The one-line reason. “A five minute call — the practice opens at 9” is doing a different job from a description: it's an imperative summary that reminds you why this is worth doing and how small it really is. One short sentence, present tense, always answering “why now?”. It is the line that gets you over the starting hump, so it is written to motivate.

Still open: whether the soft gradient ground helps or distracts — it's a tweak on this page, off by default, turn it on to compare. And whether “Show me everything” should sit as text or as a third segment.

B · THE STAR BUCKET

The header pill states the whole prize up front — ★ 5 when done — and subtasks pay out of it rather than adding on top, so breaking a task down never inflates its worth. The step meter is gone — the screen was carrying the same number three times. The header states the prize and the banked half beside it (★ 15 when done · ☆ 1 banked), and each completed step carries a hollow star of its own, so progress is legible from the rows themselves.

Size drives the number: on the card back, tap Big time and the pill counts up, Quick win and it counts down — so size reads as a value rather than a label.

B · STEP ROWS & THE TWO ACTIONS

Step rows are graded three ways: done sinks into the paper and greys out, now is a raised white card with a clay ring, later sits flat and dimmed. Only one row ever looks live.

Get more steps is the real action, so it takes a filled clay pill; Change sits beside it as plain text. A glyph before the word names an action (Change, Mark as complete, Save and next); a glyph after it means direction, which is why Get more steps keeps its trailing arrow and nothing else does. The sparkle is gone: everything here is AI, so the icon is better spent describing the action.

Both sit directly under the steps they act on — the bottom of the screen is reserved for the two terminal actions, and neither competes with Mark as complete, which is taller and shadowed. “Cut it loose” stays plain text so it never competes with complete.

B · WHAT “GET MORE STEPS” RETURNS

The ask is for three more steps each time, so the list grows by a predictable amount and the button can be pressed twice without the screen changing shape.

When the steps already on the list would finish the task on their own, there is nothing left to append — so it breaks the remaining steps down instead: intermediary steps go in among the ones still to do, the count goes up and the size of each step comes down.

Nothing is ever inserted above a completed step. Work already ticked off stays where it is, so the list only ever grows below the line you have reached. If every step is complete, there is nothing left to subdivide and the new steps go on the end.

B · A LONG LIST & THE NOTES FIELD · 05F

Everywhere else the keyboard rises over the screen and nothing moves — but the notes field is the one thing you have to see while typing, so here the page scrolls under the keyboard instead. The title and description stay put — you need to know what you're writing about — and the step list itself is what gives up the room: it becomes its own scroll area, the rows cut hard at top and bottom with a shadow so they read as sliding under an edge, with an indicator at the side, while the notes field sits directly above the keyboard. The two terminal buttons pass below the fold rather than being dimmed or removed. Dismiss the keyboard and the list is exactly where you left it.

C · AFTER THE DUMP

Parsing has one failure mode nothing downstream can catch. Triage confirms attributes — deadline, requires, size — so it never asks whether the right set of tasks exists. A line that silently didn’t become a task is invisible forever, and that’s the one that breaks the promise of “get it all out”.

A box per task, evidence underneath. Each task is a card in the title voice with the lines it was built from beneath it, in your own words — nothing reworded, only tucked under the item it was adding to. Reading down the indents is how you check the parse: every line you typed is either under a task or in a clay box saying it went nowhere.

A gate, but a cheap one. Nothing is saved until you press Add, so the screen can afford a control per task — × on the title row drops one outright, + on an unclaimed line promotes it — and the count on the button moves with both.

What did not become a task is the loudest thing on it. Unclaimed lines keep the clay dashed treatment and the quote formatting, since that is what they are — quotes with no title yet — and the list opens scrolled to them rather than to the top, because the tasks that parsed cleanly need no attention.

Promoting a line writes it a title. Press + and the parser does for that one line what it did for the rest — turns the quote into a task in the title voice, with the line it came from sitting underneath as its evidence. It becomes an ordinary box.

The change flow re-parses the whole dump. What you type is extra context for the whole dump, so tasks can merge, split or gain evidence, and the unclaimed lines are not fixed — a good clarification can leave nothing unclaimed at all. 07h shows one line promoted and one still outstanding; the count on the button and the tally beside Change these both move with whatever comes back.

D · TRIAGE MODE

Why blueprints. The triage card runs wider and taller than a deck card (336×512 against 280×392 — a taller sheet, since it carries details as well as chips) — it is a form, so it gets the room its chips need. Triage is the one mode where a card is explicitly unfinished, so the cards stop looking like paper and start looking like drawings: blueprint blue, dashed outlines, white ink, a faint grid on the ground. You can tell at a glance that you're not in the deck any more, and the dashes read as “fill this in” without a single instruction.

Each card runs the same attribute groups as the card back — size, requires, deadline — with the AI's guess as a confirmable chip and every gap dashed. Save and next advances the queue; the dot rail at the top shows how much is left, so it ends.

The title and the details are editable in place (09a). Tap the words and a caret lands where you tapped — same as a step row in edit mode. The keyboard rises over the ground rather than pushing the card up, so nothing on the card moves and the attribute groups you were reading stay where they were. The keyboard's bottom-right key reads done rather than return — a title is one line, so the key that would insert a newline is spent ending the edit instead. Details behave the same way — tap the paragraph, caret where you tapped, keyboard over the ground — so there is no separate editing screen to learn.

Where it lives on home: the badged round button sits on the expanded “Right now” sheet (01), beside capture and brain dump — one line, and the badge carries the count. The deck screens don't carry a triage entry point.

E · WHAT A CARD IS WORTH

Quick win — ★ 5. Under fifteen minutes, and you already know how to start.

Big time — ★ 20. Everything else. Two sizes only: a third would add a filter, a chip and a rule. Size is the only thing that changes what a card is worth, which is why the pill counts up and down as you tap it on the card back.

Tasks pay in fives, tidying pays nothing until the triage queue is empty — then five, once a day. Clearing a dozen cards is about a quick win’s worth of work; paying per card would reward volume and pop a +1 on every save.

A bonus is shown as a badge and the card keeps its own value, and nothing stacks: if a card qualifies twice the larger badge wins, so the value is always explainable in one sentence. Base values never fall.

E · WHY THE WINDOW OPENS EARLY

The app is aiming to land work three days before the stated deadline, so the window opens at four days out and runs two. Taken inside the window, the task finishes with a buffer. Missed, the badge is gone; the base value is unchanged, so being late costs nothing.

Short notice is not punished. A card arriving with less than four days on it opens its window immediately and still gets two days, capped at the deadline.

Offered once — the window does not come back.

From two days out, placement takes over. No badge, but the card is dealt first whatever the filter says, and the context it needs carries a dot. Taking the bonus away must not quietly demote the thing that is about to be late.

E · SKIPPING, AND WHAT IT COSTS

Two ways in: avoidance or age. Three passes while the card was genuinely in front of you, or a card that has simply been sitting there a long time. Avoidance fires first; age covers the task that is never reached.

Random in whether it fires, fixed in how much. The offer follows the size of the card — +3 on a quick win, +10 on a big time — so the amount is predictable; the coin flip decides only whether it appears when the card next comes round. From there a quick win erodes in two skips, 3 · 1 · 0, and a big time in three, 10 · 6 · 2 · 0. The loss is front-loaded, so the first skip costs the most; an escalating offer would instead pay for putting things off.

Skipped to nothing, the card sits at base, and can be offered again after a few days. Permanent loss would leave an already-avoided card worth less than it started.

One live offer at a time.

E · BANKING, AND WHAT STARS BUY

Banked stars are a progress reading: two a step on a big time card, one on a quick win, hollow until the card completes. Ten steps is the cap on a big time and five on a quick win, so the indicator fills exactly at the card’s value and never needs a rule about overflow. Completing converts the lot in one go.

Two counters, once stars are spendable: balance, which goes down when you buy, and lifetime earned, which never does. The home counter shows balance; lifetime belongs in settings.

At roughly 35 stars on a decent day — three quick wins and a big time — a first pass at prices: flourish 150 · theme 400 · bonus mechanic 1000. On that basis a first theme is about three weeks of use away.

These numbers are derived from the mechanics; there is no usage behind them yet. The ratios are the part worth defending — 4× between sizes, an offer set by size at +3 and +10, tidying an order of magnitude below doing — and the absolute values should move as soon as there is real usage to look at.

F · WHY THIS TREATMENT

Blue stops being a mode colour and becomes the colour of an unconfirmed guess — which is what triage is entirely made of, so the mode and the row now say the same thing in the same language. Worth watching that triage still feels like somewhere different: it keeps the full blueprint treatment (blue ground, grid, white ink) rather than blue rows on paper.

The treatment sits at row weight, so a guessed deadline stops outranking Details — one attribute among several rather than an interruption. Gold now means one thing only: stars.

One tick per group. Contexts can't be confirmed one at a time without three ticks in a row, so the guess is a set. That also keeps deadline and requires reading as the same pattern: label line says who guessed, the tick at the end of the group agrees.

Structure carries the link too: identical row metrics, dash weight and label pattern across the card back and the triage card, so the same component reads in both materials.

Three states, one row: blue + dashed while a guess awaits you, grey + dashed when there was nothing to guess, plain paper once the value is settled either way.

Theme. Clay is a variable: every clay tone (fills, inks, tints, borders, dark-mode variants and shadows) re-hues off one theme colour, and the in-phone surfaces off a second screen tint. Gold, pine and blueprint blue never move — they carry meaning.

S · TYPE & FORM

Type. Newsreader 500 for titles (31/36 card, 28 screen, −1% tracking) — serif carries size without shouting, which fixes the “too bold” note. Karla 400/500/600/700 for UI. DM Mono 500 for every number, date and caps label; letterspaced .07–.11em.

Radii 22 card · 15–18 field & row · 11–13 chip · 999 pill. Shadows stay warm-tinted (rgba(44,39,35,…)), never grey-black.

Buttons, three weights. Filled clay 54px with a shadow for the one terminal action per screen (Mark as complete, Brain dump, Save and next) · filled clay 40px, no shadow, for the strongest secondary (Get more steps) · plain clay-on-paper text for anything you can safely ignore (Change, Cut it loose, Skip this one). Small controls — Change on the context bar, the size toggle — are clay ink on a clay tint at 10–12 radius.

S · WHAT THINGS MEAN

Stars. Solid ★ means stars you own; hollow ☆ means stars banked on a task that isn't finished. Applies to the counter (★ 65 · ☆ 1 banked), the working header (★ 5 when done · ☆ 1 banked) and each completed step, which carries its own hollow star. “+1 today” never includes banked stars.

A bonus is shown as a badge and the card keeps its own value. The band opens with the badge and its reason — +10 bonus until Wed, +3 to start it now — and the card’s real value, 5 or 20, sits in a white pill at the other end. On the list the badge keeps its shape and the reason is dropped. Badges expire, base values never fall, and two reasons never stack — the larger badge wins, so the number is always explainable in one sentence.

Dashes mean provisional everywhere: the triage cards and their entry button, any AI guess awaiting confirmation, an unguessable deadline. Nothing dashed is settled. Blueprint blue travels with the dashes — triage, and unconfirmed guesses on the card back — so blue always means “we filled this in, you haven't agreed yet”.

Arrows. Outlined block arrows drawn on the same 4-unit shaft and 11-unit head — pointing right beside “Right now” on the collapsed bar, turning down on the expanded sheet — always in the label's own ink so they read as part of the word.

Gestures where you’re reading, visible controls where you’re editing. On a screen you are scanning — the deck, the dump receipt — a rare destructive action hides behind a swipe. Inside a mode you deliberately entered to restructure something, it is a visible control: the step list shows a grip and a bin, because hiding the mode’s main action makes the mode weaker than it looks, and because swipe and drag-to-reorder compete for the same gesture on the same row.
